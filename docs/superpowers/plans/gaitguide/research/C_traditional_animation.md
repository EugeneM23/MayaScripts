### 1.1 Why walks matter (p.102)

- Williams opens with Ken Harris's advice: a walk is "the first thing to learn", learn walks "of all kinds", and walks are hard to get right (https://s184753.blogspot.com/2019/01/more-research-animators-survival-kit.html).
- The same p.102 notes say:
  - walking is falling forward and catching yourself just in time;
  - "No two people" walk the same (same URL).
- The *Walks* mini's blurb says walks reveal the character and "tell the story" of the person (Goodreads URL above).
- That walks are the hardest thing to animate well: Engadget's app review **[snippet only]**, and "often said" per https://www.animationandvideo.com/2015/09/book-review-animators-survival-kit.html.

### 1.2 The positions

- **Contact.** Both feet on the ground. Arms are always opposite the legs, for balance and thrust (https://dominicnobledissertation.blogspot.com/2013/11/task-by-r-williams-part-2-character.html, https://cloytoons.wordpress.com/2015/12/01/animation-research-walk-cycle/).
- **Down.**
  - The bent leg takes the weight, and the body is at its lowest.
  - Williams, pp.106–107: it is the up-and-down of the masses that gives weight, and the down is "where we feel the weight" (s184753 Part 2).
  - Note-takers say the arms swing widest here (Noble, cloytoons).
- **Passing (breakdown).** The supporting leg is straight, so the pelvis, body and head ride slightly higher (Noble, cloytoons).
- **Up.** The back foot pushes off, and the body is at its highest (cloytoons).
- **Order.**
  - Williams: set the tempo, then "start with just 3 drawings" (the two contacts and the passing), then add the down and the up, then inbetween (p.111 per s184753; Noble's "recipe" lists the same steps).
  - Course versions of his "contact method":
    - On 12s: contacts on frames 1, 13 and 25; downs on 4 and 16; passings around 7 and 19; push-offs on 10 and 22 (https://animation.monmouth.edu/instruct/animation/walk-cycle/).
    - On 16s: 1 contact, 5 down, 9 passing, 13 up, 17 contact (https://tryingtobeananimator.wordpress.com/2016/10/04/introduction-to-walk-cycles/).
    - On 8s the same pattern falls on 1/3/5/7/9 (my arithmetic).

### 1.3 Tempo ("Set the Tempo", p.109)

The first job is to "set a beat". People generally walk on 12s, which Williams calls march time: half a second a step, 2 steps a second. That forces "thirds" when inbetweening, so it is easier to walk the character on 16s or 8s. Act the walk out and time it with a stopwatch (s184753 Part 2; Noble).

| Frames per step (24 fps) | Steps per second | Williams' label |
|---|---|---|
| 4 | 6 | very fast run |
| 6 | 4 | run or very fast walk |
| 8 | 3 | slow run or "cartoon" walk |
| 12 | 2 | brisk, business-like, "natural" walk |
| 16 | 1.5 | strolling, leisurely |
| 20 | ~1.2 | elderly or tired person |
| 24 | 1 | slow step |
| 32 | 0.75 | a joke line from a song for a near-dead plod (not reproduced) |

Sources: Noble; s184753; an identical uncredited list at https://www.chiefdelphi.com/forums/showthread.php?p=560637.

Watch the units in derivative sources. Monmouth's table counts whole cycles (two steps) and gets several steps-per-second figures wrong. tryingtobeananimator's "16s ≈ 3 steps a second" is also wrong: it is 1.5.

### 1.4 The passing position is the key (p.111)

- "The crucial thing is this middle position and where we put it." With the same contacts, the middle position "utterly changes the walk" (s184753 Part 2).
- A lowered passing position gives a "cartoony" walk: contacts high, passing low (Noble).
- A heavy character can stretch or squash at the passing position instead of rising; keep the pelvis level (Noble).
- **Two Ways to Plan a Walk (p.115)** **[inferred]**: Noble's notes describe two ways to build a walk: contacts low with the passing raised into the up position, or the reverse, cartoony version with the passing low. A garbled scan says the double bounce combines "the two approaches" **[snippet only]**. That fits, but it is not confirmed.

### 1.5 Double bounce (p.118)

- Williams: "2 bounces per step". The passing position goes down instead of up. It reads as energetic, optimistic, a "can do" character (Noble).
- A scan excerpt links it to jazzy, bouncing characters of the early 1930s **[snippet only]**.
- John K: the body drops at the foot contact and again "in the middle of the step". It is common in 1930s cartoons; Porky in Clampett's films always walks double bounce. His beat descriptions: 12x is easy and natural, 10x is urgent and march-like, 8x is quick and up-tempo (http://johnkstuff.blogspot.com/2007/10/10-x-beats-flip-frog-double-bounce-walk.html).
- One teaching handout puts the body down on the contact and the passing, and up on the inbetweens (https://joshuarosenstock.com/teaching/GPH213/walkcycle/walk.html).

### 1.6 Foot action (pp.136–141)

- "The heel is the lead part"; the foot is secondary and follows.
- Keep the heel flat until the last possible moment before it lifts; that is what sells weight (https://s184753.blogspot.com/2019/01/final-research-on-animators-survival.html).
- The foot trails the heel and flops down after the heel strike (https://edwardboyleanimation.wordpress.com/2016/01/04/walk-cycle-research-3-animators-survival-kit/; the "flops forward" wording is **[snippet only]**).
- The exact phrase "peels off the ground" is **[unconfirmed]**, as is any rule for foot spacing on the contacts.

### 1.7 Weight shift, belt line, counteraction (pp.146–147, 156)

- Each raised foot thrusts the body's weight forward and to the side, over the planted foot. Walking is a series of controlled falls. Shoulders generally oppose the hips. Exaggerate all of this for heavy or cartoony characters (s184753 Part 3; Noble).
- Belt line, as the notes word it: tilt it to favour the lower leg; normally it is down on the foot that is down and up on the foot that is up.
  - This is ambiguous. Westlund instead teaches that the hip on the passing side drops, with the shoulders counter-tilted (https://pushingposes.blogspot.com/2008/03/basic-walk-cycle-part-2.html).
  - Check p.147 before stating a rule.
- Counteraction, for example in a sneak the head goes forward while the hands go back (Noble).

### 1.8 Arms (p.148)

- Arms swing in pendulum arcs to balance the walk's thrust (edwardboyle).
- At the passing position the shoulder rises, the hand is at the lowest point of its arc, and the arm is straight (s184753 Part 3).
- **Where the extremes fall.** Most notes put the widest swing on the **down**, not the contact (s184753, Noble, cloytoons, tryingtobeananimator). IdeaRocket compares sources: Muybridge and the conventional teaching put the arm's extreme at the contact ("heel-toe"); Williams puts it at the "landing" (the down). The overlap makes Williams' walk look "more integrated" (https://idearocketanimation.com/2-walk-cycle/; landing = down per https://idearocketanimation.com/27285-animate-a-walk-cycle/). One blog reads Williams the other way (prefers the contact). Treat that as the outlier.
- Flexibility: "drag the hand" (s184753), and "break" the elbow even where a real elbow wouldn't bend (edwardboyle). See also the Flexibility chapter, "Breaking Joints", p.231 (TOC).
- A 3D formalisation: arm extremes lag the legs by 2–5 frames (https://education.siggraph.org/static/HyperGraph/animation/character_animation/walking/walking_excercise.htm).

### 1.9 Male and female walks (pp.102–106)

- Women: short steps in a straight line, legs close, little up-and-down. "Mr. Macho": legs apart, a lot of up-and-down (s184753; Noble).
- A Williams video clip puts the difference mainly in the knees: inward for women, outward for men (edwardboyle category page: https://edwardboyleanimation.wordpress.com/category/walk-cycle-run-cycle-and-weight-exercise/page/2/).
- Critique worth noting in a reference text:
  - the section's gay-walk anecdote and its "fashion model / ballerina / stripper" foot positions;
  - Williams' p.106 explanation that women walk one foot in front of the other to protect the crotch.

  Source: https://blog.animationstudies.org/?p=869, which recommends Preston Blair and Joanna Quinn's two-spread walk cycle (in *Drawing for Animation*) as alternatives.

### 1.10 Sneaks (pp.167–175)

- Williams names three categories of sneak (Noble). Only the "traditional" one is described in the notes I found:
  - **Timing:** 24 frames a step on average, 16 fast, 32 slow.
  - **Body:** goes back as the foot lifts. On the contact the body stays back and the head is held back (a slight overlap). The body then follows forward as the foot takes the weight.
  - **Arms:** balance rather than oppose the legs.

  Sources: Noble; https://matthewhamill706.wordpress.com/2015/12/08/sneak-cycle/.
- A Ken Harris sneak on 24s: the feet go down gently and the up-and-down is exaggerated (IdeaRocket how-to).
- The tip-toe sneak (p.173) as "between a walk and a run", feet on ones **[snippet only, OCR]**.
- A separate "back/reverse" sneak **[unconfirmed]**.

### 1.11 Cycles

- Williams: don't work out a cycle walking in place with the feet sliding back (p.109, s184753). Plan the walk progressing across the page, then cycle it. Pan mechanics are in §5.
- The "change the timing of one part" trick is **[unconfirmed]**. The nearest confirmed idea is the offset arm extreme on the down (§1.8).

### 1.12 Old masters cited

- **Confirmed:** Ken Harris (p.102); Harris's 24s sneak.
- **[snippet only]:** Art Babbitt teaching Williams to read a walker's age, mood and circumstances by watching from behind (AWN review).
- **Mentioned generally:** Milt Kahl, Johnston, Thomas (animationandvideo review).
- No specific Kahl walk example confirmed.

## 2. Runs, jumps, skips (pp.176–216)

**Sections:** Runs p.176; The 4 Drawing Formula Run p.189; The 3 Drawing Run p.192; The 2 Drawing Run p.195; The Recipe p.200; Run, Jump, Skip and Leap p.201; Skips p.209; Jumps p.212; Weight on a Jump p.213 (livlily TOC).

**Walk vs run:**
- In a walk one foot is always on the ground. In a run both feet leave the ground "for 1, 2 or 3 positions" (p.176, s184753 Part 3).
- The *Runs* mini: one frame with both feet off turns a walk into a run, and the way we run shows character (Goodreads).

**Tempo:** 4 frames a step is a very fast run (6 steps a second); 6 frames is a run (4 a second); 8 frames is a slow run (3 a second). From the tempo table, §1.3.

**Runs on 6s:**
- Normal run: feet off the ground 1 frame.
- Cartoon run: both feet off for 2 positions, with a violent arm swing.
- Realistic run: little arm action, little body bob, 2 frames off.

Sources: s184753 Part 3; Noble.

**Formula runs:**
- Runs can be done "in 4's or 3rd's" (p.180).
- Fast runs (p.181): overlap the positions slightly, vary the contacts, and allow "some foot slippage".
- In the four-pose run the down and the passing merge into one (pp.181–182).
- A hunched forward run is shown (p.182).

Source: https://theanimationmuppetblog.wordpress.com/2018/10/10/animation-strategies-walk-cycles-research/.

What the 4-, 3- and 2-drawing runs contain is **[unconfirmed]**. On ones they give 6, 8 and 12 steps a second (arithmetic).

**Lean and shape:**
- The faster the run, the more the figure leans forward, and it need not stay in balance (s184753; Noble).
- The body curve reverses on the opposite step and is straight at the passing (Noble).
- Make position 5 a different silhouette from position 1 (higher or lower), overlap positions to carry the eye, and vary the silhouettes so the run doesn't read as one leg and one arm repeating (Noble).

**Examples:** a fat man running on 6s and a young girl running on 8s (https://abigailgelston.wordpress.com/2016/01/07/animators-survival-kit/).

**Jog vs sprint:** not distinguished in the Williams notes I found. One student source: a casual run has restrained arms and a slight lean, about 16 frames; a sprint has a big lean and pumping arms (https://blogs.ulster.ac.uk/b00839206-bdes-animation/2023/11/05/researching-walk-run-cycles). This is generic, not Williams.

**Jumps (pp.212–215):**
- A broad jump starts with a run and works the anticipation in while running. The spine keeps reversing. Put "lots of lean" into the bodies. Use Muybridge for the ups and downs. Keep the arms or feet moving inside the jump so it doesn't float (s184753 Part 3).
- Two demonstration jumps of about 1.5 s each: one "Simple, clear and Solid", one with stretch, squash and delayed parts (muppet blog).

**Skips (p.209):** content **[unconfirmed]**. The usual description, a step-hop on each foot in an uneven long-short rhythm, is **[memory]**.

**"Spin-out"/zip:** found in neither the Williams nor the Blair tables of contents **[unconfirmed]**.

## 5. 2D craft

**Charts and inbetweens**
- 12s force thirds; 16s and 8s halve cleanly (p.109).
- Place the passing off-centre for a stronger arc. Favour some parts toward the previous pose: rear foot, toes, wrist, head (https://pushingposes.blogspot.com/2008/03/basic-walk-cycle-part-2.html).
- Background chapters: "History of the Chart and Inbetween" p.47, "Classic Inbetween Mistakes" p.88, "Watch Your Arcs" p.90 (TOC).

**Ones and twos**
- Moving characters are usually on twos; fast action goes to ones (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Traditional_animation).
- Many animators put normal walks on twos (https://animationexplainers.com/walk-cycle-animation/). Runs are commonly on ones (Ulster student notes).
- Williams: normal spacing on twos, far-apart spacing on ones **[snippet only]**.

**Pans and sliding**
- A cycle is a treadmill: the background moves. Planted foot drifting backward means the character is too slow; drifting forward means too fast (animationexplainers).
- The floor or background moves one stride per step, linearly (SIGGRAPH).
- A real walk's forward progress is irregular, so a cycle moved at constant speed needs per-frame correction under the heel (IdeaRocket how-to).
- **Pan increment per frame = stride ÷ frames per step.** For example, a 3-inch stride on 12s is 0.25 in a frame (my arithmetic).
- **Twos against a pan on ones:** the planted foot falls off the background on every second frame. Use ones, or a matching pan on twos (standard practice, **[unconfirmed]** by any page I opened).
- Williams tolerates "some foot slippage" in fast runs (p.181).

**Perspective**
- In profile: near foot larger and lower (John K); far foot on a higher path (White, **[snippet only]**).
- Toward or away from camera: steps and bob shrink along lines to the vanishing point while the timing stays the same **[unconfirmed]**.

**Straight-ahead vs pose-to-pose:** "Three Ways to Animate" p.61 (TOC). Walks are built pose-to-pose from the contacts (§1.2) and worked out across the page (p.109).

**Boiling lines:** no source found **[unconfirmed]**.
